abstract: >-
  Introducing letterpress printing technology to the Arabic script stripped the
  writing system of many of its characteristics. The loss of several integral
  script qualities trickled down into modern Arabic font design and typesetting
  standards, becoming part of modern typographic norms. One forgotten or lost
  characteristic of the Arabic script is the agency given to its writers to
  control the placement, shape, and composition of its diacritics, using them to
  manage negative space or create diacritical compositions based solely on the
  scribe’s aesthetic tastes. This research examines and seeks to revive these
  agencies through accessible font-development methods, producing functional
  experimental fonts usable in mainstream typesetting environments.


  The developed font family showcases the potential of lost agencies in modern
  typographic environments through mainstream font-building methods, without
  redefining working patterns or software engineering. This research is not a
  type design project but an experimental, speculative type-building project
  that explores possibilities that could arise if diacritic agencies were
  revived in mainstream typesetting and design software. The typeface utilises
  variable font axes, stylistic sets, and font layering to achieve diacritic
  agencies unavailable by default. Although the research discusses diacritics
  and short vowels, the main focus of the reintroduced agencies is the Nuqat,
  since short vowels have inherited many of their manuscript agencies.


  This research was funded by Kone Säätiö and Suomen Kulttuurirahasto (Fig. 1)

  - heading: Historical Overview
    heading_level: h2
    body: >-
      In Arabic calligraphic tradition, a dot, or “Nuqta,” is the square
      (rhombic/standing on its edge) mark drawn by dragging a reed pen, or
      “Qalam,” across the writing surface (Massoudy, 2010, p. 38). Dots are a
      quintessential part of the Arabic writing system. Different-sounding
      letters share the same base shapes, and the dots or “Nuqat”, help
      differentiate between these similar letters depending on their position
      around the base character. The dots were originally small strokes placed
      around letters, introduced in the 9th century to help readers distinguish
      between individual letters (Milo & Berry, 2002, pp. 114¬–115). These
      markers contribute not only to the aesthetics of the writing system but
      also to the legibility and readability of Arabic texts.


      In the early stages of the Arabic script’s history, the writing system
      lacked markers, dots, and diacritics. Nemeth (2023) states that fourteen
      letters represented thirty phonemes, making one character correspond to
      multiple intonations (Nemeth et al., 2023, p. 379). However, the system
      was inefficient; it relied heavily on the reciter’s ability to read
      contextually. This reliance on the reader’s knowledge of the written
      content made the Arabic script system ambiguous and inaccessible to many.
      The introduction of the diacritic system during the time of the Caliph Abd
      al-Malik ibn Marwan, first in the form of the small strokes mentioned
      above, gave each phoneme a distinctive graphic character and reduced the
      vagueness of written Arabic. Abbot (1939) credits this system to Yaḥyá ibn
      Yaʿmar and states that the diacritic approach was fully completed in the
      2nd century AH [8th century AD] (Abbott, 1939b, p. 39).

  - heading: The Shape Of The Nuqta
    heading_level: h2
    body: >-
      Ibn Muqla states that a dot, or “Nuqta,” can take two visual forms: a
      [rhombic] square or a circle (al-Qalqashandy, 1914, p. 155). However, the
      most traditional and commonly used shape is the rhombic square.


      Circular dots are used sparingly, and for aesthetic reasons, in the
      Thuluth and Muhaqqaq families. Tabbal (2023) states that circular dots
      serve as an aesthetic compromise when the written composition demands it
      or cannot accommodate the proportions and size of the regular rhombic
      square (Tabbal, personal communication, July 7, 2023). Since circular
      diacritics occupy less space than their square counterparts, they are
      employed when the design lacks the space to accommodate a regular Rhombic
      Square diacritic (Fig. 2).


      In contrast to Tabbal’s claim, some manuscript references present fully
      dotted texts with circular diacritics. One such codex is the illuminated
      Qur'an of Sultan Baybars, currently held by the British Library, from the
      fourteenth-century Mamluk period, created by the calligrapher Muhammad ibn
      al-Wahid and the master illuminator Abu Bakr. The manuscript is in a
      script akin to Muhaqqaq and fully marked with circular diacritics (Fig.
      3). A similar example is a thirteenth-century manuscript folio, attributed
      to either Egypt or Syria, currently held by The Metropolitan Museum of Art
      in New York City (Fig. 4).

  - heading: The Dot As A Diacritic
    heading_level: h2
    body: >-
      Various dots adorn the Arabic writing system; ranging from one to three
      marks, they define key functional and visual characteristics of
      legibility, readability, and writing. Different letters in the Arabic
      writing system share the same base character, or “Rasm.” These letters
      either have one to three dots above or one or two below them (Fig. 5).


      The two-dot group is traditionally two single dots arranged horizontally,
      with the two opposing vertices facing each other. However, a vertical
      arrangement is also possible for this diacritic cluster; the two squares
      sit one on top of the other so that the upper vertex of the lower dot
      faces the lower point of the upper “Nuqta.” The vertical double dots are a
      stylistic choice mentioned by Ibn Muqla himself; he stated that a person
      can either place the two marks on top of each other or horizontally on the
      same line (al-Qalqashandy, 1914, p. 155) (Fig. 6).


      The three-dot diacritic group stands above the “Rasm” and never underneath
      it in the *Arabic* writing system. The dot arrangement forms an
      upward-facing triangle, with two horizontal dots supporting the third
      mark. Ibn Muqla remarks that some calligraphers draw the three dots in a
      horizontal format over the letter “Shiin” (ش) (Fig. 7), and that is
      because the letter can easily fit the dots over it horizontally, in
      contrast to other characters like the initial form of the letter “Tha’”
      (ثـ) (al-Qalqashandy, 1914, p.156).
    nav_column: left
  - heading: Diacritic Agency
    heading_level: h2
    body: The use of Diacritics in the Arabic script extends beyond the obvious one
      of legibility. Diacritic dots employed in Arabic writing hold aesthetic
      value in written text, calligraphic compositions, and artwork, creating
      balance within textual layouts and compositions. Calligraphers and scribes
      attributed these idiosyncrasies to the dot as an agency or allowance.
    nav_column: left
  - heading: The Diacritic Separate From The Letter
    heading_level: h2
    body: Calligraphers manipulated the dots adorning Arabic letters, managing their
      placement. They moved the marks around, managing the micro-levels of
      writing and overall textual and textural design. The flexibility of
      diacritic placement is possible because dotting happens after the whole
      “Rasm” of a word is complete, making it easier for the calligrapher to
      decide where and how to place the marks. Nemeth (2023) writes that Arabic
      marks and diacritics, unlike Latin ones, remain independent of the Rasm to
      which they correspond. The positioning of the dots around the base
      characters allows significant liberties, depending on the letters
      surrounding the mark (Fig. 8) (Fig. 9). That level of agency did not carry
      over to mainstream Arabic typography. This lack of command exists due to
      the limitations of time-bound Western technologies used to design
      typefaces (check the subsection titled “**Bounding Box**” in
      **Technological Limitations).**Currently, only MaryamSoft lets users
      manipulate diacritic positioning. However, this software and its typefaces
      are not available outside the Republic of Iran*. This research conducted
      diacritic experiments with MaryamSoft and QalamBartar, documented in the
      “MaryamSoft” section of this chapter.*
